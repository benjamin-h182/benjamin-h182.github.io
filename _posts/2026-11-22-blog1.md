**Synopsis**

The rise of the Byzantine Empire is a process shaped by policies of generations of Roman Emperors, coupled with the strenuous European geopolitical landscape in the late Antiquity. Entire textbooks could be written on this matter. In this blog, I will present a high-level overview of the key players and events that birthed Byzantium as we know it, focusing on the implementation of the Tetrarchy, the ascension of Constantine, and the death of Emperor Theodosius I.

**Of Diocletian and the Tetrarchy**

The “birth” of the Eastern Roman Empire, or rather, the divide of the Roman Empire of old into its Western and Eastern halves, traces its roots to the Roman Emperor Diocletian and the Crisis of the Third Century. The Roman population had contracted by ~15% by Diocletian’s rule from Trajan’s time; the number of active legionaries rose from 250,000 under Tiberius to over 600,000 under Diocletian, further straining the fragile economy. The rapid turnover of emperors led to repetitive coining to assert legitimacy, resulting to heavy debasement of the _denarius_. The _antoninianus_, issued by Emperor Caracalla to replace the _denarius_, debased from ~50% silver purity in AD 238 to just 4% purity in AD 276. Gothic, Germanic, and Saxon raids penetrated the Empire’s northern defenses and stretched deep into imperial heartlands, burning vast fields of crops and crippling trade networks.

Following of Diocletian’s ascension, it soon became clear that Rome was too vast for any ruler to govern alone. The emperor thus implemented the tetrarchy system, dividing Rome into two Eastern and two Western administrative regions, roughly along the Illyricum – Dalmatia – Egyptus axis. This model set the geographical boundaries that would later define Byzantium’s borders with the West.

**Of Constantine and the Founding of Constantinople**

 	Flavius Valerius Constantinus, more commonly known as Constantine the Great, emerged victorious as the sole Roman Emperor in AD 306 following years of civil wars. By this time, large swaths of Italia, Gaul, and Hispania had been plundered. While the distance of Rome from the frontlines ensured its stability, it also meant the city was ill-equipped to respond rapidly and efficiently to the ever more frequent barbarian incursions and the encroaching Sassanid threat from the East. The need to establish a new administrative centre grew by the day.
	
	Byzantium, an ancient Greek city, soon caught the eyes of the emperor. Its strategic location overseeing the Bosphorus provided control over all trade networks from the Mediterranean into the Black Sea and from Asia into Thrace. The city had already been extensively rebuilt and refortified during the reign of Septimius Severus, and the city’s proximity to the East provided it with the much-needed capability to answer to threats from the Danubian and Persian frontiers.
	
	The city was founded on AD 324, dedicated on 11 May 330, and renamed _Constantinopolis_, or the city of Constantine. Thus marks the beginning of Constantinople, the Queen of cities, a bastion that would endure for the next thousand years, protecting the Roman people, their culture, faith, and Empire. Although its walls and emperor fell to the thunder of Ottoman artillery in 1453, it has forever cemented its place in history as a city of unparalleled beauty, majesty, and splendor. Renamed to Istanbul in 1930, it remains the largest city in Europe to this day and a central hub of trade, transportation, and cultural dialogue between the East and West.
